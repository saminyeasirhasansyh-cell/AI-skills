# Professional Technical Writing

Rules for producing and reviewing professional and academic documents (memos, emails, reports, papers) that busy, possibly distracted readers can skim quickly and still extract the key information.

## Core reader assumptions

Professional readers:
- Read quickly, often inattentively or with distractions.
- Look for concise, clear, tonally appropriate prose.
- Expect key information in prominent positions.
- Want visually accessible information (not dense unbroken text).

Design every document around these assumptions.

## Structure rules

1. **BLUF / inverted pyramid.** Lead with the main point, then supply supporting details. Never bury the key point or conclusion in the middle of a paragraph or section.
2. **Reveal structure visually.**
   - Use content-specific, descriptive headings (and subheadings for longer documents) — headings are a roadmap, not decoration.
   - Use bullets, boldface, and numbered lists where appropriate, but only when the list has textual context (an introductory sentence, not a bare list).
   - Leave white space around sections so the document is scannable.
3. **Topic sentences.** Every paragraph should open with a topic sentence that frames what follows. A good topic sentence does one of:
   - Makes an assertion/claim/recommendation the paragraph then supports (e.g., "The designers were not responsible for the failure.")
   - Interprets data or a finding up front, before the detail (e.g., "X is 15% faster than Y under the same conditions.")
   - Establishes the scenario/context/state the paragraph will describe.
   - Sets up an enumeration of parallel items the paragraph will cover (e.g., "The decision involves 3 significant risks: A, B and C.")
4. **Cohesion.** Use consistent key words/phrases across the document. Use logical transitions (therefore, in contrast, however, moreover, likewise, not only... but also). Use enumerative words for process/chronology (first, second, next, then). Eliminate ambiguous pronouns ("this," "it") with no clear referent.
5. **Figures and tables.** Number and caption every figure/table. Cite each one in the text — figures and text must work together, not stand alone.

## Style rules

- Keep subjects close to verbs; use precise, descriptive verbs over vague ones.
- Cut unnecessary wordiness — e.g., "blue in color" → "blue"; "due to the fact that" → "because."
- Prefer active voice over passive (e.g., "The researcher analyzed the data" rather than "The data were analyzed") — it's clearer and more concise.
- Treat each word as if it costs money: cut hedge/filler words like "quite," "definitely," "very," "somewhat" unless they carry real meaning.
- Vary sentence construction across the document; the same cadence repeated paragraph after paragraph loses the reader.
- Match tone and word choice to audience, purpose, and occasion.
- Proofread carefully; read the document aloud to catch awkward phrasing and mechanical errors.

## Formality and person (academic/professional register)

- Maintain a serious, professional tone throughout. Avoid jokes, personal anecdotes, and colloquial expressions.
- Avoid exclamation points, contractions ("can't," "don't"), emoticons, and acronyms the reader may not know.
- Write in third person almost exclusively. Avoid addressing the reader directly ("You should know...") and avoid first person ("I think this is...") — both weaken the formal tone expected in business and academic writing.
- A little repetition across sections is fine and often helpful — the same key information can appropriately appear in more than one place (e.g., intro, body, conclusion) in a longer document.

## The Executive Summary

Applies to any report-length document.

- Placed at the start; it is the most-read part of the document and must stand on its own.
- It is **not** an introduction and **not** a table of contents.
- It must state: main findings, conclusions, and recommendations/action items — condensed to the essentials a decision-maker needs.
- If the full report is highly technical and some readers are non-technical, write the summary in accessible language.
- Every point raised in the summary must be expanded upon in the body; the two should correspond to each other while each remains self-sufficient (readable independently).

Body of the report then supplies: necessary background/context, and the detailed discussion, explanations, justifications, evidence, estimates, findings, and recommendations that back up the summary — organized into sections/subsections with informative headings for longer documents.

## Document-level structure (intro → body → conclusion)

- **Introduction**: give the reader a clear map of what's coming, start to finish. This isn't a mystery novel — it's fine, even expected, to give away the ending (the conclusion/recommendation) at the beginning.
- **Outline before writing**: plan paragraph order so ideas flow logically from one to the next, rather than reading as free association.
- **Each paragraph** opens with a thesis or topic sentence stating what that paragraph is about (see Topic sentences above).
- **Conclusion**: mirror the introduction in reverse — sum up the point of the paper. Unlike the body, it's fine here to draw conclusions, add commentary, or make suggestions.

## Self-editing passes

Proofread in multiple deliberate passes, each focused on one dimension — a single read-through misses things:

1. **Content** — Does it flow logically? Is the big picture/main point clear? Are subtopics clustered together rather than scattered through a paragraph? Is sentence structure varied?
2. **Clarity and conciseness** — Are sentences clear and active-voice? Can any words be cut without losing meaning?
3. **Spelling and grammar** — Check capitalization, punctuation, spelling, word choice (e.g., where/were/wear); remove any contractions.
4. **Citation accuracy** — Is everything cited in-text also in the reference list, and vice versa? Are citations formatted correctly?

Having someone else proofread — especially for grammar/spelling and citations — is worthwhile when possible.

## Scholarly sourcing (for academic writing)

When the document needs cited sources, prefer scholarly over popular sources. A source is likely scholarly if it:
- Comes from a peer-reviewed journal.
- Follows IMRaD format (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) for empirical studies — literature reviews, theoretical articles, methodological articles, and case studies also count.
- Cites specific theories and their sources.
- Uses specialized vocabulary aimed at a scholarly audience.
- Lists author name(s) and institutional affiliation(s).
- Is published by a professional organization or academic institution.

Non-scholarly sources (general news outlets, magazines, Wikipedia, most general websites) are not appropriate as primary support in academic writing. To find scholarly sources, use a university library's databases (e.g., Web of Science, ERIC) or Google Scholar rather than general web search.

## Why write: framing, prioritization, synthesis (pre-writing / strategy documents)

Before drafting a strategy memo, analysis, or recommendation, use writing itself as a thinking tool:

- **Frame the idea** before detailing it. Answer up front: What problem are we solving? Why is it urgent, why now? What's explicitly in and out of scope? What are the goals of the solution?
- **Disaggregate and prioritize.** Complex topics have many underlying issues — writing them out helps separate and rank them. Check that the issues are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) before deciding what to focus on.
- **Synthesize, don't just summarize.** Synthesis = summary + insight — interpret what the data/findings actually mean, don't just restate them. Use this step to reconcile differing stakeholder viewpoints (e.g., engineering vs. sales vs. marketing) into one coherent recommendation.
- It's normal for some written drafts/slides never to reach the final deliverable — writing them was still valuable for clarifying the thinking behind what did make it in.

## When drafting for the user

1. If the piece is a strategy/analysis document, spend a moment framing, prioritizing, and synthesizing before drafting prose (see above) — don't just start writing from the top.
2. Identify the main point/recommendation first — write it as the lead (BLUF), not the conclusion. Draft an introduction that maps the whole document; it's fine to reveal the ending early.
3. Draft an executive summary (for report-length documents) containing only findings/conclusions/recommendations — no throat-clearing.
4. Organize the body with descriptive headings; open each section/paragraph with a topic sentence.
5. Use bullets/numbering only where a list genuinely aids scanning, and give each list a lead-in sentence.
6. Use third person and a formal register unless the user has specified a more casual house style; avoid contractions, exclamation points, and jokes/anecdotes.
7. Close with a conclusion that mirrors the introduction and is allowed to add commentary or suggestions.
8. Run the four self-editing passes (content, clarity/conciseness, spelling/grammar, citations) before calling it done.

## When reviewing the user's writing

Check against this list and flag violations concretely (quote the offending sentence, propose a fix):
- Is the main point up front, or buried?
- Do headings exist and are they descriptive (not generic like "Section 1")?
- Does every paragraph have a real topic sentence?
- Any vague "this"/"it" without a clear referent?
- Any unnumbered/uncaptioned/uncited figures or tables?
- Any wordy constructions, hedge words, or passive-voice sentences that could be tightened?
- Any first/second person, contractions, exclamation points, or unfamiliar acronyms that break formal tone?
- Sentence variety — is the same sentence pattern repeated throughout?
- If academic: are cited sources scholarly, and does every in-text citation appear in the reference list (and vice versa)?
- If there's an executive summary: does it contain only findings/conclusions/recommendations, and does the body expand on every point it raises?
