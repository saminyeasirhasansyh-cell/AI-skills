# Consulting Presentation Storyline

Distilled from ex-McKinsey/BCG/Bain practitioner guidance on how top consulting firms actually
build presentations. Use this to judge, structure, or write consulting-style deck content — pair
it with the `pptx` skill when the deliverable is an actual PowerPoint file.

## The core discipline, in one sentence

**Structure the argument before building any slide.** The storyline — what each slide's title
says, read in sequence — is the actual deliverable; slides are just the evidence layer underneath
it. Everything in this skill exists to enforce that one discipline at every level: deck, section,
slide, and sentence.

---

## 1. Deck-level structure (five sections)

1. **Frontpage.** A title of eight words or fewer, an optional one-line subheadline for further
   elaboration, the company name, and the date. Whether the deck is branded with the consultant's
   own template/color scheme (positioning the work as external) or with the client's logo and
   template (positioning it as internal work product) is a business decision upstream of the
   content — don't let it affect how rigorously the content rules below are applied.

2. **Executive summary ("At a Glance").** One slide. This is typically the single slide that takes
   the most iteration to get right, because it has to compress the entire Situation-Complication-
   Resolution argument (see §2) into something a reader can absorb standing up. Write it so that a
   reader who reads *only* this slide still walks away with the complete answer — the situation,
   the complication that demands action, and the resolution being recommended. Draft this last (or
   redraft it last), once the body storyline is stable — trying to freeze the executive summary
   before the analysis is finished usually produces a summary the analysis doesn't actually support.

3. **Body of slides.** The evidence for the storyline — in a full consulting deck this can run 50+
   slides. The two jobs of this section: (a) prove the storyline slide by slide with the minimum
   data needed, and (b) keep the *flow* between slides logical, so consecutive action titles read
   as one continuous argument rather than a series of disconnected topic dumps. See §2 and §3.

4. **Recommendation / next steps.** See §4.

5. **Appendix.** Everything relevant-but-not-essential to the main storyline: backup data,
   alternative cuts of an analysis, detail a stakeholder might ask about but that isn't needed to
   carry the argument. It is completely normal — even expected — for the appendix to be longer than
   the main deck. The discipline this enables: the main storyline stays crisp because you have
   somewhere to put everything you're tempted to leave in out of thoroughness. When editing (§7,
   step 5), the default move for a cut slide is "move to appendix," not "delete," unless it's
   genuinely redundant.

## 2. Storyline: the SCR framework

Every consulting storyline resolves to Situation → Complication → Resolution:

- **Situation** — the starting point or context: current state of affairs, background of the
  problem, or other stage-setting detail. Can also be framed narrowly around a specific opportunity
  or threat rather than a full situational overview.
- **Complication** — the specific challenge that has arisen within that situation: a roadblock, an
  unexpected development, a major hurdle, or a capability gap standing between the org and an
  opportunity it wants to capture.
- **Resolution** — the proposed solution: a clear, *actionable* plan for moving forward, not just a
  directional statement. Should include (or point to) the steps, resources, and other detail needed
  to actually execute — a resolution slide that just says "we should improve X" is incomplete.

**The action-title read-through test.** A properly built storyline should be legible from its
titles alone: strip out every slide body and read only the sequence of action titles top to bottom.
A reader should be able to say (a) what the main conclusions are, and (b) how the analysis got
there. If either fails, the storyline itself needs restructuring — don't try to fix it by rewriting
individual slides, fix the sequence first.

**How to actually build a storyline (practical process):**
- Sketch the section-level structure on paper (or in a blank Word doc) before opening PowerPoint.
- Then draft *just the action titles* for the slides you expect to need, in sequence — with rough
  notes on which sub-conclusion or analysis belongs on each one. This can live in a plain document:
  action title first, then a bulleted note underneath on the data/info that slide will need.
- Print the sequence (or use Slide Sorter view once slides exist) and read straight through. Does
  the flow make sense as a story, or does it feel like a list of topics?
- Expect to prune, reorder, and rebuild this sequence multiple times as new data comes in and as
  it's pressure-tested against stakeholders — this iteration is not a sign something went wrong,
  it's the actual mechanism by which a mediocre storyline becomes a sharp one.

### The strategy-house variant (for full strategy decks specifically)

When the deliverable is a strategy presentation rather than a narrower analysis, nest the SCR logic
inside a five-layer "strategy house," built on top of a starting-point analysis of market dynamics,
customer segments, competitive position, and internal performance:

1. **Aspiration** — purpose (why the organization exists) and vision (where it wants to be in 3, 5,
   or 10 years). This layer sets the destination that every other layer has to serve; if a later
   initiative doesn't trace back to this, question why it's in the deck.
2. **Strategic choices** — explicit *where-to-play* decisions and the must-win battles, including
   what the organization has decided *not* to do. This is where most non-consulting strategy decks
   are weakest: they present aspirational "pillars" (e.g., "Innovation," "Customer-centricity")
   instead of real trade-offs with an opportunity cost attached. If a strategic-choices slide
   doesn't name something being deprioritized or ruled out, push back on it.
3. **Strategic initiatives** — the specific programs that will win the chosen battles, each tied to
   a tangible, measurable KPI. An initiative without a measurable goal is an intention, not a
   strategy — flag this explicitly when reviewing draft content.
4. **Enablers** — the capabilities, systems, and resources that have to exist for the initiatives to
   succeed (e.g., a digital/technology enabler, a talent enabler).
5. **Foundation** — the underlying assets everything else is built on: people, culture, technology,
   balance sheet.

A complete strategy deck walks these five layers in sequence, as a story, with action titles
carrying the narrative at each layer — not as five independent, disconnected sections.

## 3. Slide-level anatomy

Every slide has exactly three parts. Nothing should appear in one part that isn't justified by the
other two — this is the internal consistency check to run on every slide:

### (a) Action title
A full sentence that states the slide's conclusion or "so-what" — never a topic label. The reader
should get the message without having to study the chart; the chart is there for anyone who wants
to verify or go deeper.

- Descriptive/topic title (avoid): *"Historical development in Revenue and Costs"* — the reader has
  to do the work of finding the insight themselves.
- Action title (use): *"Over the last 5 years, costs have grown 10% per year, double revenue
  growth"* — the message is delivered immediately; the chart underneath now serves to prove, not
  to inform.
- Consistency rule: nothing in the action title should be missing from the slide body (don't assert
  something the data doesn't show), and nothing in the slide body should be irrelevant to the
  action title (don't include data that doesn't serve the stated conclusion).
- Style rules that apply across a whole deck: keep titles to two lines or less at a consistent font
  size throughout; strip filler words ("the," "a," "in order to") to keep them punchy; make sure
  reading titles slide-to-slide, in sequence, produces the storyline read-through described in §2.

### (b) Subheading
A short, crisp line summarizing exactly what data is being shown, or adding a nuance to the main
takeaway that doesn't belong in the title itself. Examples of the right level of specificity:
*"Sales of personal luxury goods, US Market, $ billions"*, *"Forecasted evolution of battery cell
costs by 2030 ($/kWh)."* Keep it to one line; this is a label, not a second sentence of argument.

### (c) Slide body
The actual evidence: chart, table, text, and footer (source + slide number). The governing
principle is subtractive, not additive — remove every fact and figure that doesn't directly support
the action title, even if it was genuinely interesting to find during research. The temptation to
include "everything I found" is the single most common way a slide body drifts from its title.
Anything cut this way is a candidate for the appendix, not for deletion, if it might matter to a
stakeholder later.

**On writing order:** don't assume you always write the title first and then find data to match it.
In practice it's often iterative — draft a title, see what the collected data actually supports,
revise the title, check the data again — repeated until the title and the body are in exact
agreement. Treat the title as the thing worth iterating on hardest; a slide with strong data and a
weak title fails just as often as one with a strong title and weak data.

## 4. Recommendation / next-steps slides

These slides carry the actions or responses required to address the situation and complication
established earlier in the deck, typically paired with an implementation plan and immediate next
steps. Three structural conventions, in order of importance:

1. **Group recommendations into labeled categories.** Don't present a flat, undifferentiated list —
   cluster related recommendations so the reader's mental model matches the structure you intend
   (e.g., "Commercial," "Operational," "Organizational" groupings, or a near-term/medium-term/
   long-term split).
2. **Label or number each group and/or each individual recommendation.** This matters especially
   when the recommendations span multiple slides or get discussed out loud — numbering gives both
   the reader and the presenter a stable reference ("as we said in recommendation 2...").
3. **Write every recommendation in active voice, verb-first.** Start with an action word: "Grow...",
   "Minimize...", "Improve...", "Increase...", "Target...", "Diversify...", "Consolidate..." Avoid
   passive or hedged constructions like "Growth of X should be considered" or "It may be worth
   exploring Y."

Layout is flexible beyond these three rules (grid of numbered cards, a table with owner/timeline
columns, a simple prioritized list) — choose based on how many recommendations there are and
whether timing/ownership needs to be visible on the slide itself.

## 5. Formatting discipline

Apply this when actually producing slides (via the `pptx` skill or otherwise), not just when
drafting content:

- **Color.** Keep the palette simple and deliberate. Establish a color hierarchy up front (e.g.,
  one brand-neutral base color for structure, one accent color reserved for the single most
  important data point or insight on a slide) and apply it identically across every slide in the
  deck — don't let color choice vary slide-to-slide based on what "looks nice" locally.
- **Fonts.** Pick one, or at most two, font families for the whole deck and never deviate (e.g., one
  font for titles, a second for body text — not more). Consistency here is one of the highest-
  leverage, lowest-effort things separating a polished deck from an amateur one.
- **Margins.** Never let content cross the slide margins. Use guides/gridlines while building so
  this is enforced automatically rather than checked after the fact.
- **Titles.** Two lines or less, uniform font size across every slide in the deck — a title that's
  suddenly three lines or a different size on one slide is an immediate visual tell that something
  was rushed.
- **Lists.** Default to bullets. Only use numbered lists when the number itself carries meaning
  (e.g., a ranking, a sequence of steps) — using numbers just to enumerate unordered points is a
  common but avoidable tell.
- **Icons.** Use icons to replace bullets on otherwise-simple slides, but only where the icon
  genuinely represents the content next to it (a supply-chain icon next to a supply-chain point, not
  a generic checkmark everywhere). Keep every icon in the deck the same visual style and weight —
  mixing icon styles is as damaging as mixing fonts. Reuse the same icon later in the deck when
  referring back to the same topic, so icons build visual continuity across the storyline.
- **Alignment.** Every recurring element — title position, subheading position, logo, page number,
  source line, column headers, chart axes — should occupy the exact same position on every slide
  where it appears. Flipping through the deck, nothing structural should visibly shift. A well-built
  master template/slide master is the most reliable way to enforce this rather than eyeballing it
  slide by slide.
- **Animations.** Avoid fancy transitions and animations entirely; they add nothing to a data-driven
  deck and read as unpolished in this format.
- **Slide number and source.** Every content slide needs both a slide number and a source citation
  for any data shown, placed in the same footer position throughout.
- **Text.** Review every slide's text for unnecessary words and sentences; the goal is the shortest
  phrasing that still fully carries the point — this is an editing pass, not a first-draft skill.
- **Visuals.** Charts, tables, diagrams, and images must be high enough quality to add real value,
  not decoration. Add callouts or highlights directly on a chart wherever it makes the so-what more
  immediately visible (e.g., circling the one bar that matters, annotating an inflection point).
  Visual styling (colors, fonts, layout) must match the rest of the deck exactly.

## 6. How consulting firms actually produce decks this fast (useful context, not just rules)

Understanding *why* these decks reach this standard clarifies which of the rules above matter most
and how to compensate for not having the underlying system:

1. **A library of past cases.** Consultants rarely start a storyline from a blank page — they pull
   the two or three best decks from similar past engagements for the storyline sequencing, how a
   sensitive recommendation was framed, and what belonged in the appendix. When helping someone
   build a deck, ask whether they have a past deck (their own, or a close comparable) to use as a
   sequencing reference before drafting from scratch.
2. **A library of complex slide layouts.** Individual slide layouts are pulled from a proven
   collection and then filled with this case's data — very few slides in a real deck are designed
   fresh. When producing slides, prefer reusing a layout pattern already established elsewhere in
   the same deck over inventing a new one, unless the content genuinely doesn't fit.
3. **Apprenticeship in layout judgment** (why a comparison belongs in a table vs. a chart, etc.) is
   trained through repeated review cycles. Absent that apprenticeship, default to the simplest
   layout that fully carries the message — a table for exact comparisons, a chart for trend/
   magnitude, a matrix for two-dimensional positioning — and be ready to explain the choice if
   asked.
4. **Explicit training in techniques like action titles and SCR storylining**, reinforced in every
   review cycle — which is exactly what §2–§3 of this skill exist to substitute for.

## 7. Using AI well on a consulting deck (don't produce an "AI-looking" deck)

### Why AI output defaults to generic
A language model predicts the statistically most likely continuation given its training data. The
most likely output is, by construction, the *average* of everything similar it has seen — which is
the opposite of a differentiated, situation-specific argument. This isn't a fixable quirk of any
particular tool; it's a structural property of how the output is generated, and it's the reason the
seven patterns below show up reliably across AI-generated decks regardless of which tool produced
them.

### Seven failure patterns to watch for and correct
1. **Generic content** — no point of view specific to this exact situation; reads like it could
   apply to any company in the category.
2. **No strategic thinking** — jumps straight to slide production instead of starting from a
   day-one hypothesis that the analysis is meant to prove or disprove.
3. **No storyline iteration** — sudden jumps in logic between slides, or several slides that
   essentially restate the same point in slightly different words, because there's no slide-to-slide
   continuity being enforced. A human storyline gets pruned and rebuilt repeatedly as new data
   arrives and gets pressure-tested out loud; AI output tends to accumulate more slides instead of
   revising existing ones.
4. **Overfilled slides** — the model defaults to thoroughness, packing bullets and graphics onto
   every slide, which is the most visible tell of an unedited AI deck. White space is not empty
   space in a consulting slide — it's what makes the one message stand out.
5. **Hallucinated numbers and conclusions** — stated with full confidence, which is what makes them
   dangerous: they read as authoritative and get anchored on rather than questioned. Every figure
   needs an independent source check before it's trusted, and watch specifically for the
   anchoring-and-adjustment trap — once a wrong number is on the slide, it's tempting to tweak it
   rather than re-derive it from scratch.
6. **Fluffy writing, not insight** — wordy phrasing, filler openers ("in today's fast-paced
   world..."), sophisticated-sounding language that doesn't actually engage the specific constraints
   of the case. Consulting writing is the inverse: precise, minimal, and situation-specific. Editing
   text down to the minimum that still carries the full so-what is itself a distinct skill worth
   applying deliberately, not a byproduct of "cleaning up."
7. **Missed small layout details** — an AI-produced slide can look 80% finished but miss exactly
   the details covered in §5 (white space balance, the no-fly-zone around content, font-size
   balance, which element is visually emphasized). These details compound; individually each seems
   minor, but together their absence is what makes a slide feel subtly "off" even when nothing is
   overtly wrong with it.

### The 6-step build process — what to keep human vs. delegate to AI

| Step | What it is | Who does it | Detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Audience & outcome | Decide who the deck is for and what specific action/decision it should produce (a go-decision, a shared view of the situation, a budget approval) | **Human, always** | A model cannot decide this. Also consider, as a human: can this audience actually deliver the outcome you need? What objections will they raise, and what evidence in the deck will need to pre-empt them? Use AI only to pressure-test an answer you've already reached, never to supply it. |
| 2. Storyline structure | The section-level skeleton the whole deck hangs on | **Brainstorm with AI, human decides** | Ask for 2–3 candidate section-level structures for the specific audience and outcome (e.g., "give me an SCR-based storyline and a pyramid-principle-based storyline for X"). Treat every output as raw options, not a draft to accept. Keep pruning, shuffling, and rebuilding as data comes in, and test the storyline with real stakeholders before treating it as final — this step doesn't finish after the first pass. |
| 3. Slide-level draft | Sketching individual slides: a first take on action titles and a rough sense of what data each slide needs | **AI drafts, human reacts** | This is a legitimate, efficient use of AI — a scaffold to edit against is faster than starting each slide from a blank page. The output here is disposable scaffolding, not content to ship. |
| 4. Fill in content and data | Building each slide: inserting real data, writing final text | **Delegate to AI, human sources and sanity-checks everything** | This is normally the single biggest time sink in the whole process, so it's where AI assistance pays off most — but the request should be "suggest a strong way to show this specific point" or "draft a first pass of the text/data for me to adjust," never "generate the whole deck." Always find and verify the underlying data yourself; never take a generated number at face value. |
| 5. Ruthless editing | Cutting anything that doesn't move the story forward; merging or removing slides that duplicate a message; checking every title against the overall structure | **Human decides, AI as a second reader** | Ask AI to flag action titles that don't fit the overall storyline, and to surface slides that carry roughly the same message so they can be merged or cut. The human makes every final call — this step is explicitly one of the most overlooked, highest-value skills in deck-building, and it's the step most likely to get skipped if a single AI prompt is used to "finish" the deck. |
| 6. Hygiene checks | Spacing, alignment, font consistency, spelling/grammar, missing footnotes or sources | **AI sweep** | This is the step AI is most reliably good at — genuinely comparable to a careful human detail pass, and worth fully delegating. |

### The single most important failure mode to avoid
Collapsing steps 2, 3, and 4 into one prompt — "generate the whole deck from this data/topic." This
removes the critical thinking that should happen in step 2, triggers the overfilling/hallucination/
fluff problems from step 4, and typically costs as much cleanup time afterward as building the deck
correctly from the start would have. Keep steps 1, 2, and 5 firmly in human hands in every case; use
AI to accelerate steps 3, 4, and 6, always with human sourcing and a human final read.

## 8. Avoiding the visual "AI-generated" look

§7 covers *content* signals that reveal AI generation (hallucinated numbers, fluffy prose, no
strategic thinking). This section covers the separate, purely *visual* signals that make a deck
look automated even when the content is solid — worth checking independently, because a deck can
pass every §7 check and still visually read as machine-made.

### Six visual markers audiences recognize as "AI made this"
1. **Repetitive structural patterns** — every slide uses the identical layout (title top-left,
   bullets center, image bottom-right, repeated slide after slide) regardless of what the content
   actually is. Predictability is what signals automation here, not any single layout being bad.
2. **Generic, interchangeable visual treatment** — nothing about the design relates to this
   specific content; the same visual approach would work equally well for a completely different
   topic. This signals no design thinking happened.
3. **Cookie-cutter aesthetics** — a look pulled from a limited, widely-reused template set, which
   audiences start to recognize across many different presentations they've seen before.
4. **Mechanical element positioning** — text and visuals land wherever a layout algorithm puts
   them, not where visual hierarchy or flow would put them; nothing guides the eye deliberately
   through the slide.
5. **No design personality or point of view** — every choice is "safe" and neutral; nothing
   reflects a specific stylistic judgment or intention, so the deck feels devoid of authorship.
6. **No content-driven variation** — a data-heavy slide, a comparison slide, and a vision/aspiration
   slide all get the same visual treatment, when each type of content actually calls for a different
   layout logic (a data slide needs a chart-forward treatment; a comparison needs symmetric visual
   weighting; a vision statement needs more white space and less clutter).

### What actually fixes this
- **Vary layout by content type, not by rotation.** Choose the layout for each slide because it
  fits *that* content (a table for an exact comparison, a chart for a trend, generous white space
  for a single big idea) — not because it's "the next template in the sequence." This is the same
  judgment call described in §6, point 3 (layout judgment), applied at the visual-design level
  rather than the content-structure level.
- **Build in a deliberate visual hierarchy and eye path.** Before finalizing a slide, ask: where
  does the eye land first, and does that match the single message the action title states? If the
  eye has no obvious entry point or wanders without a clear path, the layout is mechanical, not
  designed.
- **Add specificity that a generic template cannot fake.** Real photographs or screenshots instead
  of stock images; a company's actual logo, brand colors, and visual identity applied consistently
  (not a generic default theme); named client/project references and concrete data drawn from this
  situation rather than illustrative placeholders. Specificity is what signals human authorship —
  a generic AI pass has no way to know these details unless it's explicitly given them, so feeding
  it this detail up front (per step 4 of the process in §7) is itself part of avoiding the generic
  look, not just a polish step done afterward.
- **Make visible, deliberate edits after any AI-assisted draft.** Swap a generic image for a
  relevant one, adjust which element is emphasized, rewrite text to match the actual voice being
  used, nudge a layout to fit this specific case better. These are small changes, but they're what
  demonstrate a human genuinely engaged with the draft rather than accepting it wholesale — the
  same principle as the human-owned editing step (step 5) in the 6-step process in §7.
- **Don't treat "looks automated" as a content problem only.** A deck can have a sharp SCR
  storyline, rigorous action titles, and fully sourced data, and still visually read as AI-made if
  every slide uses the identical mechanical layout. Run the content checks in §7 and the visual
  checks in this section separately — passing one doesn't mean the other is fine.

### Where this matters most
Investor presentations, client proposals, board and executive briefings, competitive/pitch
situations, and anything else that will be shown outside the immediate team. In these contexts, a
visually generic or obviously automated deck can read as a lack of care or sophistication regardless
of how sound the underlying analysis is — which is exactly the situation this whole skill exists to
prevent from happening on the content side; the visual dimension deserves the same level of
attention, not less.

## Quick self-check before calling a slide or deck "done"

- Do the action titles alone, read in sequence, tell the complete story — situation, complication,
  resolution?
- Is there exactly one message per slide, and does every element on the slide support that message?
- Is every number sourced and independently sanity-checked, not just plausible-sounding?
- In a strategy deck: are the strategic choices explicit about what the org will *not* do, not just
  a list of aspirational pillars?
- Does every strategic initiative have a measurable KPI attached, not just a description?
- Are recommendations grouped, labeled/numbered, and written verb-first in active voice?
- Is every recurring element (title position, logo, page number, source line) in the exact same
  place on every slide?
- Would cutting this slide change the argument? If not, cut it or move it to the appendix rather
  than leaving it in out of thoroughness.
- If this content came from an AI draft: has it been checked against all seven failure patterns in
  §7 before being treated as finished?
- Does the deck's *layout* vary meaningfully by content type, or does every slide use the same
  mechanical structure regardless of what it's showing? (§8)
- Would a reader be able to tell this deck apart from a generic template-based one — real
  examples, real branding, a deliberate visual hierarchy — or does it feel interchangeable with any
  other deck on the same topic? (§8)
